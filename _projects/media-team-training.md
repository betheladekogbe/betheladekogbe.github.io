Working in live production has taught me that the success of a team depends on more than the equipment it uses. The people operating it need opportunities to learn, ask questions, and grow in confidence.

At Pentecostal Tabernacle, I helped lead a volunteer media team supporting weekly services, conferences, and special events. Alongside coordinating technical production, scheduling, and creative projects, I wanted to make it easier for new volunteers to gain the practical skills they needed to contribute.

## Training in a live-production environment

One part of that work was developing a **four-service, hands-on onboarding program** that introduced approximately **15 volunteers** to live media production. Instead of relying only on an initial explanation of the equipment, the approach paired new volunteers with experienced operators during actual services.

This gave incoming team members a chance to learn in context: seeing how camera operation, video switching, and live-production responsibilities fit together, while having someone experienced nearby to guide them.

It also gave existing volunteers a meaningful role in developing the next group of operators. That kind of peer-to-peer support was important to how I approached the team.


## Inside the orientation guide

The onboarding program was supported by an **11-slide orientation guide** introducing the media team's responsibilities, expectations, training opportunities, and day-to-day operations. The material covered production, audio, and social media and communications, so volunteers could see how their work fit into the broader team.

One of the most useful visuals was a shadowing model with four stages marked **0%, 30%, 70%, and 100%**. It illustrated a gradual increase in hands-on participation rather than expecting a first-time operator to take over immediately. These percentages describe the training stages shown in the guide, not measured completion rates.

## Creating opportunities to learn

Beyond regular onboarding, I organized dedicated learning opportunities for media volunteers. In May 2026, those included camera-operator workshop sessions scheduled for **May 7 and May 14**, along with a **photography workshop on May 9**.

The planning involved more than picking dates. I coordinated volunteer communications, schedules, and workshop information so participants knew what was happening and how to take part.

## Leadership behind the scenes

The training work was connected to my broader production leadership responsibilities. I coordinated volunteer schedules and assignments, helped prepare teams for services and events, and communicated across production, worship, and creative teams.

That combination of people development and day-to-day production coordination shaped how I approached the role: a team should be prepared for the next service, but its members should also have room to build skills that will serve them beyond it.

## What I took from the experience

A well-run production matters, but helping someone become capable of running one is just as meaningful to me. This experience brought together two parts of my work that I want to keep developing: **creative and technical production**, and **teaching and mentoring people**.

The four-service onboarding structure and the dedicated workshop opportunities reflect the kind of work I want to keep doing: creating learning experiences while supporting the people who make productions happen.
